How to Get Your First 100 Users for a Free Web Tool

Updated 2026. Reading time: about 12 minutes.

You spent weeks building it. The code works, the design is clean, the tool does what you promised. Then you press publish, refresh the analytics page, and see two visitors, one of whom is you.

Almost every small maker lands here. Building a tool and finding people who need it are different skills, and only the first one gets taught. Free tools often die a few weeks after launch because nobody wrote down the second part.

The first 100 users are easier to find than the first 10,000. You don't need a budget, a following, or a growth hack. You need a clear description of what the tool does, a few places where sharing is welcome, and enough patience to talk to the people who show up. What follows is the order that tends to work for one person working alone.

Why 100 matters

A hundred users sounds small. It's the point where guessing stops.

With a hundred people using the tool, you can see which features get used and which get ignored. You read what they write when something breaks. You notice that half of them arrived from one place, which tells you where to spend your time. A few will recommend the tool without being asked, and those recommendations do more than any ad you could buy.

The other benefit is harder to measure. A tool with a hundred users feels real to the person who built it, and that feeling is what keeps you working through the quiet weeks.

Step one: check the basics before you show anyone

Makers often start promoting before the tool is ready, and the first visitors leave with an impression that's hard to fix. Spend an evening on the following.

Open the tool on your phone. A large share of visitors will be on a small screen, and a layout that looks right on your laptop can be unusable there. Check that the buttons are easy to tap and the text is easy to read.

Run the main task from start to finish the way a stranger would, without your own shortcuts. If the tool converts files, upload a messy real file instead of the clean sample you built it with.

Watch the loading speed. If the first screen takes more than a few seconds, people close the tab before they see anything. Compress images, remove scripts you don't use, and test on a slow connection.

Make it obvious how to reach you. A short contact page or an email address in the footer tells visitors a person is behind the tool, and it gives them somewhere to report a bug instead of leaving.

Add a privacy page, even a plain one. If the tool processes files, say whether they stay in the browser or go to a server. Plain language about what happens to someone's data earns trust faster than a badge.

Set up basic analytics. You need to know how many people visit, which pages they open, and where they came from. Without those numbers you can't tell which effort worked.

Step two: pick who the first hundred are

"Everyone" is not an audience. A message written for everyone reads as if it were written for no one, and nobody recognizes themselves in it.

Pick one kind of person who gets the most from the tool. A freelancer who needs to send invoices. A student converting a file before class. A shop owner compressing product photos. A developer cleaning up messy data every day.

Write one sentence describing that person and their problem, something like "freelance designers who lose time resizing images for each platform." That sentence decides everything after it: the tagline, the screenshots, the communities you visit.

If you aren't sure who your audience is, look at the people who already found you. Check the search terms in your analytics, reread any messages you got, and think about who asked you to build the thing. The answer is often someone much like you, because makers tend to build what they needed themselves.

Step three: let the first screen do the work

A visitor decides in a few seconds whether to stay, and the tool itself is the best argument you have. Put it on the first screen.

Long introductions, big banners, and sign-up boxes push the working part out of view. Someone who came to compress an image wants a place to drop the image, not three paragraphs about your mission.

Keep the headline plain. "Compress images in your browser, no upload" describes what happens. "The ultimate image optimization platform" describes nothing.

If the tool works without an account, say so near the top. Plenty of people search specifically for tools that don't ask them to register, and "No sign-up needed" removes the worry before it appears.

Show a sample result if you can. People understand a tool faster when they can see what comes out of it.

Step four: write a listing people click

Nearly every method in this guide depends on the same short text: a name, a tagline, a description. It's worth an hour of your time.

The name should be easy to say and easy to remember. If it doesn't describe the tool, the tagline has to carry that job.

The tagline says what the tool does in one plain line. Compare "AI-powered next-generation productivity solution" with "Turn meeting notes into a clear to-do list." The second one tells the reader what they get.

The description answers three questions: what it does, who it's for, and why it's simpler or different from the alternatives. Say whether it's free and whether an account is needed, and keep it short enough to read in ten seconds.

Use a clear, square logo. If you don't have one, put your initial on a clean background. A real logo makes a listing look less like a placeholder.

Write several versions and read them out loud. If a line sounds like an advertisement, rewrite it the way you'd explain the tool to a friend.

Step five: launch on directories

Directories are the gentlest source of early visitors. People who browse them are already looking for new tools, so you don't have to convince them to care.

Start with the ones that welcome small makers and don't require a following. Product Launch Directory lets you list a free tool in a couple of minutes without an account: paste the address, check the details, publish. Visitors can browse by category, upvote, and comment, and you get a private key for editing the listing later.

Then look for directories that match your kind of tool. Some cover AI tools, some cover developer utilities, some cover indie projects. Submit to a handful of the relevant ones instead of dozens of random ones.

A few habits make these launches work better. Have the text and logo ready so every submission is consistent. Keep a note of where and when you submitted, so you can check back. Reply to every comment, even if it's just a thank you. Be honest about what the tool can't do.

Don't expect one listing to bring hundreds of visitors. Expect a slow trickle of curious people, a couple of useful comments, and occasionally a link from another site. The returns add up across several listings.

Step six: join communities without spamming them

Communities are full of people with exactly the problem your tool solves. They also spot pushy self-promotion fast and remember it.

Be a member first. Spend a few weeks reading, answering questions, and giving advice before you mention your own work.

Pick communities built around your audience rather than around makers. A tool for teachers belongs where teachers talk. A tool for developers belongs in developer forums. A tool for shop owners belongs in the groups shop owners use.

When a question comes up that your tool can answer, write a helpful reply first. Explain the solution in a few sentences. Then, if it fits, mention that you built a free tool that does it and leave the link. A reply that helps with or without the link is almost always welcome.

Read the rules before posting. Some communities ban promotion outright, some allow it on a particular day, some require you to say you made the thing. Follow them exactly; one careless post can get you banned and follow your name around.

Don't post the same message in many places on the same day. People notice, and moderators notice sooner.

If there's a section for showing projects, use it. Write a short post about what you built, why, and what you want feedback on. A post that asks a real question gets answers; a post that only announces something gets silence.

Step seven: answer the questions people search for

Search is slow to start and keeps working while you sleep. A young site can still win small, specific searches.

Think about what your user types into a search box, and think about the problem rather than your tool's name. "Convert HEIC to JPG without installing anything." "How long does an audiobook take at 1.5x speed." "Free invoice generator no account."

Build a page for each of those questions. Each page explains the answer in plain language, shows how to do it, and offers your tool as the quickest route. Write for the person reading it. A page that genuinely answers a question usually ranks above a page stuffed with keywords.

Give the page a title and description that match the question, and use headings so both readers and search engines can follow it. Link related pages to each other and to your home page.

Expect a wait. A new page can go weeks without traffic, and the first visitors will be few. Good pages keep working, though, and ten of them can bring steadier traffic than any launch day.

Keep the technical basics in place: fast loading, a layout that works on phones, and a sitemap so search engines can find your pages. The free submission tools search engines offer help them find a new site sooner.

Step eight: ask for feedback and use it

Early users are more useful as teachers than as a number. Anyone who tries the tool and tells you what confused them has saved you weeks of guessing.

Make it easy to send a message. An email address or a short form is enough, and don't require an account to leave a comment.

Answer quickly and personally. A friendly reply within a day or two surprises people, because most small sites never answer, and that reply is often what turns a stranger into a regular user.

Ask specific questions. "What were you trying to do when you visited?" and "What almost made you leave?" produce better answers than "What do you think?"

Look for repeats. If three people mention the same problem, it isn't a coincidence. Fix it and tell them you did, since people like seeing that their message changed something.

Keep a list of requests and complaints in a text file. Read it once a month and decide what to improve next.

Step nine: ask your own network

Friends, classmates, colleagues, and online contacts are the obvious first audience. Asking feels awkward, but a specific, respectful request usually gets a better response than you expect.

Skip "Please check out my project." Say who you think would benefit and ask a clear question instead. "I built a free tool that compresses photos in the browser. Do you ever need to resize images for work? I'd like your honest opinion."

Ask people to try the tool, not to promote it. Feedback from ten honest people is worth more than a forced share from a hundred, and if they like the tool they may share it anyway.

Make passing it on easy: a clean link, a short description, a good preview image. If your tool produces a result, a share button for that result helps.

Send one friendly message and leave it there. Nobody should feel guilty about saying no.

Step ten: share what you're building

Plenty of small tools grew because the maker talked openly about the work. People follow a project that's moving and remember who's behind it.

You don't need an audience or a polished writing style. Short updates are enough: what you built this week, a problem you solved, a number you're pleased with, a mistake you made. A real account of a bad week holds attention better than an announcement.

Pick one or two places where your audience already spends time and post there regularly. A steady trickle beats a burst of posts followed by silence.

Attach something useful to each post: a lesson, a before-and-after screenshot, a tip. Then mention the tool with a link near the end.

Thank people in public when they help. It costs a sentence and makes the next person more willing to speak up.

Step eleven: measure what's working

Trying many things at once makes you feel busy without teaching you anything. A few numbers keep you honest.

Track where visitors come from. Most analytics tools show the source of each visit. Compare after each effort: did a directory listing bring five visitors, did a community post bring fifty? That's where your next hour goes.

Watch what people do after they land. If most leave within seconds, the first screen isn't matching what they expected. If they use the tool and don't come back, think about whether there's a reason to return.

Count people, not page views. One visitor refreshing the page fifty times is still one visitor. Count the ones who completed the main task.

Write down what you tried and what happened, with a date and a place. The notes stop you repeating a mistake and show you which effort actually moved the numbers.

Ignore the daily swings. Traffic moves for reasons you can't see, so judge by the trend over a few weeks.

Mistakes that cost the most time

Some of these come up often enough to name.

  • Promoting before the tool works. A broken first impression is difficult to repair.
  • Doing everything at once. Three channels done properly beat ten done badly.
  • Spamming. The same link everywhere brings a short spike, a damaged reputation, and sometimes a ban.
  • Ignoring feedback. When people repeat themselves, they're handing you a to-do list.
  • Vague taglines. If a visitor can't tell what the tool does in five seconds, they've left already.
  • Expecting speed. Most small tools grow slowly at first; a few visitors a day is a normal start.
  • Quitting at week two. A lot of makers stop right before the effort starts returning something, and consistency is worth more than any single clever move.
  • Buying traffic or votes. It brings no real users, can get a listing removed, and teaches you nothing about your audience.

A thirty-day plan

If a schedule helps, here's one to adjust around your time and your tool.

Week one is preparation. Test on a phone. Fix the first screen. Write the tagline and description. Set up analytics and a contact address. Make a simple logo. Write the one sentence about your ideal user.

Week two is listing. Submit to three or four relevant directories, Product Launch Directory among them. Note each submission. Reply to comments. Post once about the launch in a community that allows it.

Week three is people. Spend time in the communities where your audience is. Answer questions without pushing the tool. Message a few people who might benefit and ask for honest feedback. Fix anything you hear twice.

Week four is something that lasts. Write two or three pages answering questions your audience searches for, and link them to the tool and to each other. Post a short update about what you learned. Read your notes and choose two channels for next month.

Done steadily, a hundred users is realistic within the first month or two for some tools and takes longer for others. Either is fine as long as each week leaves you knowing more about the people you're building for.

Tools that are hard to promote

Some tools are harder to promote because they solve a rare problem or look like a dozen others. A few adjustments help.

Narrow the focus. A tool that does one job for one group is easier to describe and easier to pass on than a tool that does everything.

Find an angle. Maybe it runs entirely in the browser, needs no account, works offline, or supports a language other tools ignore. Lead with it.

Write a usable example: a short guide showing the tool in a real situation, so a reader can picture themselves using it.

Look for a partner. Another small maker may serve the same audience with a different tool, and recommending each other honestly, with no deal attached, gets both of you in front of new people.

Keeping the ones you get

A hundred visitors is half the job. The other half is giving them a reason to come back.

Make the tool reliable. One that works every time earns trust, so fix bugs quickly and don't break the main feature to ship something new.

Keep it light. No pop-ups, no forced newsletter, no extra steps. Someone who came for a small task appreciates a small tool.

Offer an optional way to stay in touch, such as an email update or a link to your progress. Never force it, and make leaving easy.

Say thank you, on the page, in replies, in public. It's a sentence, and it makes people feel part of the project.

The bigger picture

No hidden trick gets you to a hundred users. A clear message, a tool that works, a few places where sharing is welcome, and a willingness to listen do it. Makers who reach that number usually get there by repeating ordinary things for long enough.

If you're at zero, or two, that's where everyone started. Make one thing clearer today: a better tagline, one listing on a site that welcomes small projects, a real answer to the first comment you get. Then do it again tomorrow.
